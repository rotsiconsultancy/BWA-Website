It is a dark stone at first glance. Deep green, almost black in low light. Then you look closer and see them: small red flecks, scattered through the green like sparks from a fire.

People have been telling stories about those sparks for two thousand years.

## A stone that turned the sun red

The old name for bloodstone is heliotrope, from the Greek for "sun-turner". The name comes from a Roman story.

In the first century, the writer Pliny reported what some people of his time believed: drop this stone into a bowl of water in the sunshine, and the sun's reflection turns blood-red. Some said that with the right prayers, carried together with the heliotrope plant, the stone could even make its wearer invisible.

Pliny thought that last claim was nonsense, and said so. But he wrote down where the best stones came from, and one of the places he named was Æthiopia, in Africa.

## Drops of red

In medieval Europe, Christians looked at the red flecks and saw something else. They said the spots were drops of Christ's blood. It is easy to see how the name bloodstone stuck.

## Into battle

Stones like this were carried as amulets for a very long time. One of the most moving survives in London's Science Museum: a bloodstone amulet carried by a soldier in the First World War.

We don't know his name. We know that he went into one of the worst wars in history, and that he took a small green and red stone with him.
