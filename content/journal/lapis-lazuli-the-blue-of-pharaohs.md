Hold a lapis lazuli bead up to the light and look closely. Deep blue, a few pale patches, and scattered through it, tiny specks that shine like gold.

People have loved that look for thousands of years. It sat on the face of an Egyptian king. It was ground into the most expensive paint in Europe. And it still turns heads on a wrist today.

## What is lapis lazuli?

Lapis lazuli is a rock, not a single mineral. It is a mix of several minerals pressed together. Three matter most:

- **Lazurite** gives the blue. It is the main ingredient in good lapis.
- **Calcite** shows up as white or grey patches and streaks.
- **Pyrite** makes the gold-coloured flecks.

Pyrite is often called fool's gold. It has a metallic shine, but it is not gold at all. Many people think a light sprinkle of it makes lapis look better, like stars in a night sky. Too much, and the stone starts to look dull.

The name is old too. *Lapis* is Latin for stone. *Lazuli* comes, through Arabic, from the Persian name for the gem. The same root gave English the word *azure*, and Spanish and Portuguese the word *azul*, blue.

## Where lapis comes from: the mines of Badakhshan

For most of history, the best lapis came from one place: the mountains of Badakhshan, in the north-east of Afghanistan. The mines at Sar-i Sang there have been worked for more than 6,000 years. They are still producing lapis today.

Think about that. Long before anyone wrote things down, people were climbing into those mountains to dig out blue stone and carry it across the world.

## Lapis in ancient Egypt

Egypt had no lapis of its own. Most scholars believe it travelled from Badakhshan, thousands of kilometres away, passing from trader to trader along the way. That made it rare and precious.

The most famous example is the gold mask of Tutankhamun, the young king who ruled about 3,300 years ago. Look at its face and you see dark blue lines around the eyes and in the eyebrows. That blue is inlaid lapis lazuli.

## Ultramarine: the paint worth more than gold

Lapis did not only go into jewellery. In Europe it was ground down to make a blue paint called ultramarine. The name means "beyond the sea", because the stone came from so far away.

Making it was hard work. Simply crushing lapis gives a dull, greyish powder. Painters had to wash and refine it to pull out the pure blue. The best ultramarine was so costly that it could be worth more than gold, and people paying for a painting sometimes set out in the contract how much of it the artist had to use.

So painters saved it for what mattered most. In many Renaissance paintings, the brightest blue is the robe of the Virgin Mary. That blue is lapis.

## Lapis lazuli vs sodalite: how to tell them apart

Sodalite is the stone most often confused with lapis. It makes sense: the blue mineral in lapis, lazurite, belongs to the sodalite family. But sodalite is its own stone, and there are a few clues to tell them apart.

- **Gold flecks.** Lapis often has pyrite. Sodalite rarely does. If you see gold sparkle, lapis is the more likely answer.
- **The blue.** Sodalite's blue tends to lean darker and more violet, closer to indigo.
- **The white.** Sodalite often has white veins and patches running through it. Lapis can have white calcite too, which is why the gold is the better clue.

## Dyed lapis and imitations

Because good lapis is valued, the market is full of variations. Some lapis is dyed to deepen its blue or to hide white calcite. Some is treated with wax or oil to improve its shine. And other stones, such as jasper or howlite, are sometimes dyed blue and sold as lapis. There is even man-made material designed to look like it.

## Wearing and caring for lapis

Lapis is a soft stone. It sits around 5 to 6 on the hardness scale, softer than quartz. It is fine for daily wear, but treat it kindly:

- **Clean it gently.** Warm, soapy water and a soft cloth are all it needs.
- **Skip the chemicals.** Keep it away from perfume, cleaning products and solvents. Some dyes and wax coatings can come off with harsh chemicals or heat.
- **Store it apart.** Keep it away from harder stones and metal that can scratch it.

## What lapis lazuli is said to mean

Lapis lazuli is traditionally associated with wisdom, truth and royalty. Part of that comes from its history: it was worn by kings and set into the treasures of pharaohs. Sodalite, its paler cousin, is often linked with calm and clear thinking.

These are beliefs, not proven effects. Wear lapis for the meaning, or simply for that deep blue with its specks of gold. Either way, you are wearing the same colour that once looked out from Tutankhamun's mask.
