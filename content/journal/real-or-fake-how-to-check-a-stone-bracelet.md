You found a bracelet you like. Maybe you pointed your phone at it and asked Google what stone it is. Now you want to know the next thing: is it real?

It is a fair question. Glass, plastic and dyed stones are everywhere, and a photo cannot always tell them apart. But a few gentle checks, done at home with nothing more than your hands and your eyes, will tell you a lot.

## What "real" actually means

There are three kinds of bead you will meet:

- **Natural stone:** cut and polished from rock, like tiger's eye, lava or howlite.
- **Dyed stone:** still natural stone, but coloured to look brighter or to look like a different stone.
- **Man-made:** glass, plastic or resin, made in a factory to look like stone.

None of these is wrong to wear. What is wrong is selling one as another. A dyed stone sold as dyed is honest. Blue glass sold as lapis is not.

## Six gentle checks you can do at home

None of these will harm your bracelet. No single check is proof on its own, so use them together.

### 1. Feel the temperature

Pick the bracelet up after it has been sitting in the room. Stone and glass feel cool against your skin at first. Plastic feels close to room temperature and warms up quickly in your hand. This check is good at catching plastic. It will not tell stone from glass.

### 2. Feel the weight

Plastic beads feel light, almost empty. Stone and glass feel heavier for their size.

There is one famous exception: lava. Lava stone is full of tiny holes left by gas bubbles, so it is real rock that feels light. Our **Longonot Black** is a good example. Pick it up and the lightness surprises people, but look closely and you will see the pores of real volcanic rock.

### 3. Look for natural variation

Nature does not repeat itself. In a real stone bracelet, every bead is a little different: the bands sit in different places, the colour shifts, one bead is darker than the next.

Tiger's eye shows this well. Turn a bead in the light and a soft band of shine moves across it. In real tiger's eye, that band and the stripes behind it change from bead to bead. Glass imitations made from fused fibres often give a band that looks too sharp and too even, the same on every bead. Our **Simba Gold** is tiger's eye: look at it bead by bead and no two match.

### 4. Look for bubbles and seams

Hold a bead up to good light, with a magnifying glass or your phone camera zoomed in. Small, perfectly round bubbles inside a bead are a sign of glass. A thin line running around the middle of each bead, or rough edges at the drill holes, point to a moulded bead, usually plastic or glass. Note the other side, though: good glass can be made with no bubbles at all.

### 5. Think about the price

A bright blue "turquoise" bracelet at a very low price is a warning sign. Natural stones vary in price, but if a famous, sought-after stone is selling for the price of plastic, ask why.

### 6. Ask the seller

This is the easiest check of all. A good seller will tell you plainly what the beads are, whether they are dyed, and whether any of them are man-made. If the answer is vague, that tells you something too.

## How dyeing works

Some stones are porous: full of tiny spaces too small to see. Soak them in dye and the colour moves into those spaces. Soft, porous stones are the ones most often dyed.

### Howlite: the white stone that becomes "turquoise"

Howlite is naturally white with grey to black veins, the look of our **Batian White**. Because it is porous, it takes dye easily. Dyed sky blue, its veins look a lot like turquoise, and it is one of the most common turquoise look-alikes. That blue howlite is still real howlite. The problem only starts when it is sold as turquoise.

### Lapis lazuli and turquoise

Lapis lazuli is one of the stones most often dyed, to deepen its blue, and turquoise is dyed often too. Natural lapis usually shows small white streaks of calcite and golden flecks of pyrite. A perfectly even, inky blue with no streaks at all deserves a second look.

### Lava

Lava is dyed too, because its pores soak up colour easily. Red and orange lava can also be completely natural: iron in the hot rock rusts as it cools. Our **Suswa Rust**, **Menengai Rust** and **Moto Lava** are natural in colour, not dyed. If you ever wonder about a lava bracelet, ask.

## Stones that are often imitated

### Goldstone: beautiful, and man-made

Goldstone is glass. The classic brown kind sparkles because tiny copper crystals form inside the glass as it is made. Blue goldstone gets its colour from cobalt instead. The recipe goes back to glassmakers in 17th-century Venice. The sparkle is spread through the glass more evenly than flecks in a natural stone ever are, and that is one way to spot it.

### Magnetic "hematite"

Natural hematite is a heavy, metallic grey stone, and at most it is weakly magnetic. The shiny beads that snap onto each other like magnets are almost always a man-made material, often sold as hematine. If a hematite bracelet clings to a fridge or to itself, it is very likely man-made.

## A careful dye check, if you want to go further

If you really want to know whether a stone is dyed, there is one more check, but it is not completely gentle. Do it only on a bracelet you own, and only if you accept a small risk to one bead.

1. Choose a hidden bead, one that sits under the clasp or against the inside of your wrist.
2. Dip a cotton bud in acetone (nail polish remover).
3. Rub it quickly on that one bead, keeping the liquid away from the cord.
4. Wipe the bead straight away with a damp cloth.

Colour on the cotton means the bead is dyed, or not natural stone at all. A clean cotton bud does not prove the colour is natural, though, because some dyed stones are sealed with wax. Acetone can also dull the spot you tested, which is why a hidden bead matters. For most people, asking the seller is the better first step.

## The short answer

Real stone feels cool, varies from bead to bead, and rarely looks perfect. Dyed stone is still stone, as long as nobody pretends otherwise. Man-made beads can be lovely, as long as they are named for what they are. The best check of all is a seller who tells you the truth before you ask.
